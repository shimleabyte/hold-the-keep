# Hold the Keep — Visual Reference Board

This is a study board, not a source-asset library. References establish construction logic, material behavior, clothing, silhouette, pixel-grid discipline and atmosphere. Do not trace, copy or extract another game's sprites, character designs, textures or UI.

## 1. Architecture and settlement construction

### Zunzgen Büchel motte-and-bailey reconstruction — Archaeology Baselland
[Reference and archaeological context](https://www.archaeologie.bl.ch/entdecken/fundstelle/65/fuerstengrab-kultplatz-oder-burg/)

![Reconstructed medieval wooden tower and palisade](https://www.archaeologie.bl.ch/uploads/images/fundstellen/fotos/Zunzgen_Buchel7.jpg)

Study the relationship between raised defensive position, timber palisade, narrow access, work buildings and ordinary rural activity. The important lesson is the functional hierarchy: tower and enclosure dominate; small structures support life within the enclosure.

### Königspfalz Tilleda open-air museum — Harz Information
[Reconstruction of the 10th–12th century imperial palace](https://www.harzinfo.de/erlebnisse/poi/freilichtmuseum-koenigspfalz-tilleda)

Study the differences between stone and timber buildings, roof mass, modest openings, paths and settlement spacing. The site is earlier than the game's primary clothing reference period, so use it for construction and massing rather than as a strict late-medieval costume model.

## 2. Clothing, armor and working equipment

### Material culture of the medieval countryside, 13th–15th century
[Research panel and published proceedings](https://www.ruralhistory.eu/pdfs/rh2019book.pdf)

Study the practical layering of garments, second-hand clothing, climate adaptation and the gradual shift in male clothing forms during the 14th century. Rural clothing should not resemble a modern standardized work uniform.

### Fashion in European Armor, 1300–1400 — The Metropolitan Museum of Art
[Metropolitan Museum essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/fashion-in-european-armor-1300-1400)

Use this for the relationship between clothing fashion, mobility, protection and armor construction. It is useful for preventing armor from becoming a disconnected fantasy costume.

### Hauberk / mail shirt — London Museum
[Late 14th–early 15th century object record](https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/v/object-36460/hauberk-mail-shirt/)

Study the actual linked-ring construction and its labor/material cost. The museum notes the importance of padded textile protection for many soldiers; mail should not be the default clothing of every guard or peasant.

### Mail shirt — Wallace Collection
[Late 14th–early 15th century example](https://wallacelive.wallacecollection.org/eMP/eMuseumPlus?module=collection&objectId=60492&service=ExternalInterface)

Use the ring scale, overlap and aged steel/copper-alloy treatment as a material reference. The object also demonstrates how armor could remain in service, be repaired and be reused.

### Tools and implements — National Museum of Slovenia
[Archaeological collection](https://www.nms.si/en/collections/online-collections/archaeological-collection/middle-ages/11852-tools-and-implements)

This collection page describes excavated iron implements, woodworking tools, augers, knives, whetstones and evidence of household textile production. Some listed objects are from the 5th–6th century; use the collection as a construction reference for simple tool forms, not as proof of a particular late-medieval date.

## 3. Pixel-art production and composition

### The Last Spell — official art-direction devlog
[The Last Blog #10: Art Direction](https://lastspell.com/art-direction/)

Study the team's move from painted concepts to artwork designed directly on a constrained pixel grid. The useful production lesson is to judge the art at the final pixel scale and preserve control over the result at the actual game zoom.

### The Last Spell — official concept-art devlog
[The Last Blog #18: Concept Arts](https://lastspell.com/the-last-blog-18-concept-arts/)

The developers identify Into the Breach as a readability influence and discuss the importance of strong pixel-art references. Use this as a reminder that tactical readability and atmosphere must coexist.

### Darkwood — developer discussion
[Developer AMA with Acid Wizard Studio](https://www.reddit.com/r/Games/comments/5hx0tf/were_afraid_to_play_horror_games_so_we_quit_our/)

Study the developers' account of frame-by-frame character work, hand-drawn character construction and the top-down view's ability to create atmosphere through uncertainty. The technique is a reference for authored identity and restraint, not a request to copy Darkwood's palette or character art.

### Darkwood — developer interview
[Culture.pl interview with co-creator Artur Kordas](https://culture.pl/en/article/bringing-out-the-fear-in-players-imaginations-a-chat-with-artur-kordas-co-creator-of-video-game)

Study how limited perspective, environmental composition and understatement can produce tension without filling the screen with spectacle.

## 4. Translation into Hold the Keep

| Reference lesson | Project application |
|---|---|
| Defensive hierarchy and palisade construction | Make the Keep, towers, walls and gates read from their footprint and approach |
| Rural garment layering | Differentiate civilian roles through garment mass, wear and posture |
| Armor as layered engineering | Use padded textile, mail and selective plate according to rank and function |
| Direct-to-grid pixel authoring | Draw at frame scale; do not downsample painted illustrations |
| Top-down uncertainty and restraint | Keep night, fog and ruin scenes atmospheric without sacrificing unit readability |
| Tactical silhouette clarity | Distinguish factions and enemy archetypes in grayscale and at 1× |

## 5. Review questions

When reviewing a new asset, ask:

- What is its construction, and what material is each part?
- What would identify it if all colors were removed?
- What would identify it if the held tool or crest were hidden?
- Does the form make sense from the game's top-down camera?
- Does it remain legible at 1×, not only in an enlarged editor preview?
- Does it tell the player something useful about function, profession, rank, danger or region?
